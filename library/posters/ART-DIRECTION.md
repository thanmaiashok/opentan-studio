# Poster art direction — learned from the 75 owner references

Source: `posters/.codex/skills/poster-art-director/references/images/` (copied from ~/posters) (cards in `references.json`).
These are the owner's taste. Every poster prompt follows this file. Generated posters in `~/posters`
(milk splashes, golden-hour packs, Diwali diya rows, plush fox mascots, teal swirl wallpapers) are the
counter-examples: they are what happens when these rules are missing.

## The grammar (what all 75 share)

1. **One flat, saturated field.** One colour fills the poster, usually the product's own colour
   (Tabasco red on red, Babor green on green, lemon can on yellow). A seamless studio backdrop or
   painted paper, not a scene. Alternative: one hard complementary slam (orange can on electric blue,
   red cocktail on royal blue). Real locations only when the place *is* the idea (rollercoaster, seaside).
2. **One hero, real and sharp.** The product is photographed, front label readable, edges crisp,
   material true (condensation on cold cans, sesame on bagels, paper fibre on torn edges). It fills
   40–70 % of the height, usually tilted 10–25° for energy, or dead centre for calm.
3. **Hard, honest light.** Direct flash or sun with short crisp shadows, or a soft studio key with a
   clean contact shadow. Never golden hour, never sun flare, never god rays, never bokeh orbs.
4. **Type is architecture, or it is whisper-small.** Either the headline is enormous — letters taller
   than the product, filling 40–70 % of the poster, the product overlapping them (Sprite, Coke, Bagel,
   Twist, Run, Fight Club) — or it is one small, widely spaced caps line with lots of empty field
   (Tabasco, Heinz). Never medium-sized type politely placed in a corner.
5. **Type voices used:** ultra-condensed heavy grotesk; extra-heavy wide grotesk; fat rounded/bubble
   display; chunky 70s retro serif; high-contrast editorial serif; thin wide-spaced serif over heavy
   condensed sans; hand-lettered brush (rare). One display voice + one small utility sans.
6. **Copy is short and witty.** 1–3 words or one confident sentence: *Bagel Time!*, *Munch!*, *Twist*,
   *Beware the heat*, *No one grows ketchup like Heinz*, *Tear into the flavor*. Wordplay on the product
   is the norm. Then small designed micro-copy: specs, a 3-item benefit list, a price badge, a date —
   aligned to a grid at the corners, tiny, never paragraphs in the middle.
7. **One idea (operator), not an effect stack.** The families:
   - *giant-type hero* — product in front of / woven through a giant word (13 refs)
   - *witty object* — product becomes something else that says the line (extinguisher, tomato stack, rope, rollercoaster, pixel teaser)
   - *torn reveal* — torn paper or a torn strip on the pack shows the flavour inside
   - *ingredient macro* — the ingredient fills the frame edge to edge; product nestled or outlined
   - *still life with flash* — monochrome set in the product colour, hand entering, long hard shadows
   - *hand-held* — a hand raises the product into a saturated sky
   - *editorial print / collage* — paper grain, halftone, grid, duotone, pasted photo panels
   - *culture collage / graphic* — real Indian material culture (truck art, mehendi, carpets, nimbu-mirchi, Devanagari type), maximal but built around one calm product
   - *film portrait* — person on a flat colour field, title taller than the person
   - *photo play* (own region, refs 51 + 53–61, 63–66, 68) — a real photo plus one hand-made layer:
     lettering wrap · doodle overlay · surreal scale · sticker cover · type in place
   - *digital break* (own region, refs 64, 69–75) — screen language cut into a real photo:
     pixel glitch · grid swap · interface frame · type window · retro screen · floating UI
8. **Props are only the product's own truth.** Lemons for a lemon drink, raspberries for raspberry —
   cut, dewy, with real physics. Splashes only with a cause (ice dropped in a glass). Never generic
   particles, sparkles, smoke wisps, floating leaves.
9. **Edges and crop do work.** Fruit and ice bleed off the corners; the field stays clean around the
   hero; information sits at the four corners on a grid.
10. **Finish:** clean commercial photography for product families; paper grain, halftone, offset
    misregistration and scuffs only for editorial/retro families. Film grain allowed (Absolut, Tequila).

11. **Lettering wrap (refs 51, 53–57).** The letters are made by hand — marker, brush, chalk paint
    on grass, inflated 3D tubes — never a font. They have depth: some letters pass behind the head,
    some cross the chest or arms, so the person sits *inside* the words. Uneven baseline, mixed filled
    and outline letters, one accent colour for small doodles (dots, ticks, pixel icons). The photo
    stays real and unretouched (flash at night, overcast street, top-down lawn, fisheye sun); the
    lettering is the only graphic element. Corner micro-copy (issue date, a 2-line manifesto) is allowed.
12. **Indian everyday objects as the joke (refs 49, 52).** A plastic chair, cutting-chai glasses —
    common objects given a voice (floating in the sky, talking in speech bubbles). Hinglish copy, short.
13. **Repetition as type (ref 50).** One word repeated as a full-width block is a valid giant-type move.
    Retro film-poster painting + offset-shadow display caps carry the festival without diyas or gold.
14. **Event flyers (refs 40, 43, 48).** The warm-to-cool wall in 48 is real light on a wall with crisp
    palm shadows — not a gradient blob or a teal-orange grade. The hero is a human gesture (a toast).
    Details stack bottom centre in one small sans with one colour bar for the time.

15. **Photo play — the other five families (refs 58–61, 63–66, 68).** Same law as lettering wrap:
    the photo is real and stays honest; only ONE playful layer goes on top.
    - *doodle overlay* (65, 66): flat cartoon doodles with faces, hearts, stars, motion lines grow out
      of the real food or clothes; a white cut-out outline where photo and drawing meet.
    - *surreal scale* (58, 61, 68): one scale break, shot like a clean studio photo — a person on their
      own giant head, a head on a toy body, a person inside a giant shopping bag. Copy goes on
      the props (receipts, tags, rulers, edition badges).
    - *sticker cover* (59, 63): a cut-out subject with a thick white border, stickers and pin badges,
      halftone on a flat field, giant title behind, metadata (date, issue, barcode) in the corners.
    - *type in place* (60): the words are a physical object (inflatable, painted) in a real place, seen
      from straight above; no person needed.
    - *floating UI* (64, now under Digital break): the client's real app screens as clear glass cards in 3D around a person,
      at different depths, some blurred near the lens. Real UI and logos only — for SaaS clients.
16. **Slop-list exceptions these refs allow.** A tactile clay or felt character (63) is fine when it is
    the subject and the texture is matte and real — glossy Pixar plastic is still banned. Glass with
    light streaks (64) is fine only on floating UI cards.
17. **Film posters (62) and street stories (67).** 62: a flat colour wall, a tight funny portrait, a giant
    faded word behind, the title in native script. 67: a real local street, people small, a tiny
    bilingual hand-lettered title and film-style credits — the quiet version of culture editorial.

18. **Digital break (refs 64, 69–75).** The screen language is the idea, and it touches ONE place:
    - *pixel glitch* (69, 74): square pixels or datamosh blocks replace only the face or eyes; the rest
      of the portrait stays sharp. A second image (a city) can live inside the glitch strip.
    - *grid swap* (70): one subject on a strict grid, every cell the same pose in a new material
      (x-ray, chrome, thermal, paint). Info lives in the empty outer cells.
    - *interface frame* (71, 75): real UI chrome is the frame — design-tool selection boxes, a phone
      share sheet. The subject is sharp inside the frame or breaks out over the UI. Frosted glass
      outside the boxes is allowed here.
    - *type window* (72): huge letters are a mask onto the photo; the subject and one prop break past
      the letter edges onto plain paper. Fisheye and low angle add the push.
    - *retro screen* (73): an old beige computer as stage, a hand or person reaching out of the
      screen, paper-collage cut-outs and print dust around it.
    - *floating UI* (64): see rule 15.
    For SaaS work use the client's real UI and logos, never invented screens.

## People and likeness
Refs 50, 53–62, 64–69, 72, 74, 75 show real people. Copy the format, never the face: use the client's own photo, a
`ref character` image, or an invented person. Never ask the model for a celebrity or film star.

## Never (the slop list)
Golden-hour backlight · sun flare · god rays · bokeh balls · splashes or floating packs without cause ·
glossy plastic CGI · teal-and-orange grade · glowing particles/sparkles · 3D plush or Pixar mascots ·
gold serif festival templates (diya rows + rangoli + gold "Happy …") · gradient blob backgrounds ·
small headline top-left over a busy scene · lifestyle clutter (leaves, windows, tables) behind a pack ·
redrawn/invented logos or pack text · more than one idea.

## Brand assets
- Never let the model invent a logo or pack label. Attach the real pack/logo image (`--ref`) and say
  "reproduce this pack exactly as photographed". If fidelity still fails, keep the pack small/turned or
  leave the logo out and say so.
- Non-Latin copy (Kannada, Hindi): ask for a real script-native typeface, give the exact string, keep it short.
