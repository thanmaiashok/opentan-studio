# Research Practice: Building Taste Into Decisions

Use this guide when the user supplies websites, reels, archives, moodboards, designers, studios, eras, or asks for stronger taste. The goal is not to collect more images. It is to turn close study into decisions that improve the current brief.

## The depth rule

Study one useful target deeply before browsing broadly. A target can be one designer, studio, campaign, archive theme, movement, decade, format, or typeface in use.

Start with a **research question** tied to the job, for example:

- How do successful beverage posters make one pack feel monumental without losing appetite?
- How does a miniature-world image prove scale through camera, depth of field, contact, and material?
- How do 1960s cultural posters create authority with only two inks?
- How is an editorial serif paired with utility type in real packaging?

Do not call a research pass complete because thumbnails were saved. Open the work at useful size, read available credits and context, and inspect enough related pieces to identify a repeated grammar. One excellent outlier is worth recording when it challenges that grammar.

## The study loop

For each serious reference, capture:

```text
Source and creator:
Date/context/format:
Rights status: reusable / reference-only / unclear
Communication job:
First read → second read → proof/CTA:
Grid and dominant gesture:
Image/camera/physics logic:
Color relationship:
Type roles and behavior:
Material/process:
Why it holds together:
Transferable principle:
What must not be copied:
```

Then synthesize the set:

1. Name the repeated decision, not the repeated surface style.
2. Name the strongest outlier and why it still works.
3. State which principle answers the current product truth or message.
4. State the design distance needed to avoid cloning.
5. Carry the chosen principle into the art-direction DNA and discard the rest.

Prefer causal language: “the high-angle view plus a narrow band of focus makes a full-size scene read as a miniature” is useful; “dreamy, cinematic, premium” is not.

## A working archive, not a moodboard graveyard

Keep a living reference channel or directing sheet with a reason for every saved work. Revisit it after production and record whether the transferred principle survived thumbnail, full-size, and crop tests. Remove references that contributed no decision. This turns research into a learning loop instead of a folder that is never reopened.

## Source stack

Use primary or well-documented archives when they fit the research question. Treat archive imagery as reference-only unless the item page explicitly grants reuse.

| Need | Starting source | Use it for | Important boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Modern American poster, identity, and illustration history | [SVA Archives](https://archives.sva.edu/) | Milton Glaser, SVA poster history, creator-led collections | Educational viewing does not grant reuse rights |
| Cross-disciplinary design, pattern, object, and textile history | [Cooper Hewitt Collection](https://collection.cooperhewitt.org/) | Pulling composition and material ideas from outside graphic design | Check each object's rights and image status |
| Typography in real contexts | [Fonts In Use](https://fontsinuse.com/) | Type selection, pairing, format, industry, and period comparisons | Examples document use; they do not license the underlying work or font |
| Overlooked print and graphic-design history | [Design Reviewed](https://designreviewed.com/) | Movements, countries, decades, periodicals, packaging, and scanned artefacts | Free study is not permission to lift scans |
| Inclusive, vernacular, regional, and process-rich histories | [People's Graphic Design Archive](https://peoplesgdarchive.org/) | Countering a narrow canon and following unexpected lineages | Contributors retain rights; verify item provenance |
| Named consumer aesthetics since the 1970s | [CARI](https://cari.institute/) | Locating an aesthetic in cultural and commercial context | CARI explicitly positions artifacts for research/reference, not unauthorized redistribution |
| Japanese graphic design and major designer archives | [DNP Graphic Design Archives](https://www.dnpfcp.jp/foundation_e/archives/) | Ikko Tanaka, Shigeo Fukuda, Kazumasa Nagai, and postwar Japanese design | Much material is for study; verify access and reproduction rights |
| Public-domain fine art with authoritative records | [National Gallery of Art Open Access](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/free-images-and-open-access) | High-resolution painting, print, and historical image research or licensed reuse | Confirm the individual work and media are marked open access; never imply museum endorsement |

Search by a question-bearing combination—creator + format, decade + country, typeface + industry, movement + material—rather than generic “best design inspiration.” For type, study the face doing an actual job. For cultural or regional work, include sources from the culture and preserve creator credits.

## Reel and social-reference handling

Social posts are leads, not authorities. Record the creator, caption, visible technique, and publication context. When playback is unavailable, do not invent the unseen frames; use the cover/caption as limited evidence and strengthen the lesson with primary archives or perception research. Never treat engagement numbers as a quality score.

## Exit condition

Research is ready to direct when it yields:

- one verified product or subject truth;
- one human meaning or communication job;
- one transferable visual principle;
- one primary visual concept operator or a justified decision to stay direct;
- one attention path;
- one rights boundary;
- and one sentence explaining why this direction belongs to this brief.

If the output is only a list of adjectives, links, or styles, the research is not finished.
