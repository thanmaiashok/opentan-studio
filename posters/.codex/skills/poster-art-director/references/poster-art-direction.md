# Poster Art Direction: Initial Visual Language

This is the distilled art direction from the first supplied reference set. It is a decision guide for original work, not a request to copy any poster, campaign, logo, or exact composition.

## North star

Simple but meaningful. The viewer should understand the subject quickly, feel a point of view immediately, and discover craft in the details. The most successful references make one decisive move—an object becomes a metaphor, typography becomes architecture, a product is staged as a character, or a culturally specific material becomes the world around it.

## The poster grammar

1. **Idea before surface.** Write the concept as a sentence: “A small bottle behaves like a fire extinguisher,” “the product cuts through the page,” or “the subject is held inside a pattern from its world.” If the idea is weak in words, effects will not rescue it.
2. **Hero before decoration.** Give one object, person, or typographic phrase the first read. Secondary motifs should frame, interrupt, echo, or explain it.
3. **One dominant gesture.** Choose a primary structure: oversized headline, centered hero, grid, torn reveal, radial field, patterned border, stacked object, or photographic action crop. Supporting devices must obey it.
4. **Hierarchy through scale and contrast.** A useful default is headline/hero first, message second, brand/product proof third, micro-copy last. Make the order visible without relying on reading every word.
5. **Controlled tension.** Pair opposites deliberately: quiet cream with violent red, flat type with tactile photography, strict grid with an object that breaks it, documentary subject with fashion-grade product framing, or local material language with contemporary typography.
6. **Material honesty.** Choose a medium and stay coherent: clean studio ad, screen-printed collage, distressed editorial, retro catalog, cut-paper construction, or photographic campaign. Mixed media can be excellent, but the joins must feel authored.

## Color and texture

- Start with a two- or three-color relationship and add neutrals for breathing room. Strong families in the references include red/cream/black, yellow/black/red, cobalt/green/cream, pink/cobalt/red, and acid green/deep green.
- Warm neutrals, paper white, brick red, tomato, saffron, cobalt, leaf green, and charcoal create a tactile editorial base. Use saturation as emphasis, not as a default for every layer.
- Paper grain, halftone, photocopy contrast, scratches, imperfect ink, and soft shadow make digital elements feel placed in a physical world. Keep the texture scale consistent; giant grain over a tiny logo looks accidental.
- A product’s physical cues—condensation, metal rim, gloss, pulp, leather, fabric, smoke, or label paper—can do more brand work than extra copy.

## Typography

- Use display type as a structural element: behind the hero, cropped at the edge, split by a tear, or made large enough to become the field.
- Condensed bold sans gives urgency and poster authority. Heavy geometric sans gives modern product confidence. Editorial serif gives film, luxury, or literary gravity. Hand-drawn or vernacular lettering can deliver warmth and place when it is genuinely appropriate.
- Combine at most two or three type voices. Establish clear roles for headline, supporting copy, and metadata. Avoid decorative fonts that compete with the hero.
- Tune tracking, line breaks, baseline alignment, and optical centering by eye. Do not accept automatic centering when the image has asymmetric visual weight.
- Keep tiny copy sparse and legible. Micro-copy should reward inspection, not become a texture block that the viewer cannot parse.

## Image and object treatment

- Product posters benefit from a hero object with a readable silhouette, believable perspective, contact shadow, and a clear relationship to the background. Close crops, condensation, ingredient pairings, and unexpected scale can make a familiar product feel fresh.
- Editorial and film posters can use portraits, halftone, red/black duotone, torn strips, or typographic framing, but the treatment should reveal character rather than merely distress the image.
- Sport and fashion posters gain energy from motion, low perspective, off-center crops, suspended limbs, oversized shoes, or type that collides with the action. Keep the body and product readable.
- Collage works when edges, lighting, and grain agree. Give every cutout a deliberate edge treatment—clean, paper-torn, halftone, or shadowed—rather than mixing accidental halos.

## Product-type modes

| Product or subject | Strong starting mode | What to protect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beverage, food, or condiment | Ingredient/product metaphor, studio hero, tactile surface, short punchline | Label fidelity, appetite, condensation/gloss, clean silhouette |
| Fashion, footwear, or sport | Action crop, oversized type, color block, motion or low-angle perspective | Product recognizability, body language, energy, logo restraint |
| Film, series, or cultural idea | Symbolic portrait, editorial grid, duotone, torn/collage reveal | Character, tone, title hierarchy, emotional tension |
| Lifestyle or local product | Pattern/material world, vernacular detail, restrained modern type | Specificity, respect, legibility, connection to place |
| Premium object or automobile | Quiet field, precise crop, catalog/grid system, monochrome or limited palette | Proportion, finish, silhouette, negative space |

## Production rules

- Follow the active output mode. For this workspace's complete-image generation mode, include supplied brand assets and exact text in the art-directed generation brief, then regenerate when fidelity fails; do not repair the image with an undisclosed post-generation layout pass. If public-facing accuracy cannot be achieved, report it instead of presenting approximate type or marks as exact.
- Preserve supplied logos without stretching, re-drawing, or adding effects that reduce recognition. Keep clear space unless the brief deliberately calls for an integrated lockup.
- Design at the final aspect ratio and leave bleed/safe margins when print output is intended. Check both the full-size poster and a small thumbnail.
- Do not clone a reference. Transfer the underlying decision—hierarchy, contrast, metaphor, material, crop, or cultural cue—to a new subject and message.

## Final art-direction questions

- What is the one idea the viewer remembers?
- What is the first thing seen at thumbnail size?
- Does every element earn its place?
- Is the palette doing emotional work or only adding noise?
- Is the product/subject more desirable, legible, or memorable because of the composition?
- Are the logo, label, copy, script, and margins exact enough for the intended use?

## Second-batch expansion: creativity, color, physics, and reveal

The next references add a more commercial and experimental product language. They sharpen the system in five areas.

### 1. Color composition is the engine

Color can carry the entire mood when the palette is disciplined. Strong approaches from this batch include:

- **Monochrome product world:** green product, green rope, green field; vary value, texture, and type weight so the image does not become flat.
- **Complementary impact:** cobalt and orange, cyan and orange, red and pink, or yellow and blue; let one hue dominate and reserve the other for the product, headline, or action cue.
- **Analogous appetite:** pink/red/peach, orange/yellow, or cream/coffee brown; ideal for food, beverage, warmth, and indulgence.
- **Neutral plus one signal:** cream/black with gold, pale blue with one red berry, or a quiet field with a single saturated object; useful for premium or suspenseful work.
- **Color as information:** badges, labels, side notes, and UI-like markers can reuse the hero color to create a visual system instead of random decoration.

Choose the relationship before choosing individual hex values. Protect contrast between the hero, the type, and the background; saturation without hierarchy is noise.

### 2. Physics can explain the product

Treat the product as an object with mass, tension, temperature, surface, and consequence. A rope can restrain or frame a tube; a can can ride a rollercoaster; a drink can splash through type; food can wrap around a word; fruit can function as architecture.

Use one of three physics modes:

1. **Observed physics:** believable gravity, contact shadow, condensation, reflections, splash, fabric, and material response.
2. **Staged physics:** a constructed scene that is physically plausible but unusually composed—floating slices, arranged fruit, an object held against sky, or a carefully balanced stack.
3. **Concept physics:** an intentional violation—pixelation hides the product, a label becomes a visual window, type is wrapped by food, or the product occupies an impossible scale. The violation must reveal a message, not just show off.

The more impossible the scene, the more disciplined the light direction, silhouette, edge, and shadow need to be.

### 3. Reveal and suspense are compositional tools

Do not always show the product in full. Use torn paper, a pixel mask, a slice of ingredient, a cropped label, a partial silhouette, a blocked face, or a shadow to make the viewer complete the idea. Reveal should be staged in layers:

- **Signal:** color, silhouette, texture, or a recognizable brand cue.
- **Clue:** ingredient, use case, material, or action.
- **Payoff:** the full product, message, or visual metaphor.

For public-facing product work, preserve enough brand and label evidence to avoid confusion. Suspense should increase curiosity without sacrificing recognition.

### 4. Aspect ratio changes narrative

- **9:16 / tall:** use vertical movement, stacked type, a product rising or falling, ingredient cascades, or a reveal from top to bottom. Keep the main read inside a central spine and use the edges for atmosphere or supporting information.
- **4:5 / 2:3:** use a hero object plus supporting ingredients, a strong headline field, or a portrait/product relationship. This is the most flexible poster proportion.
- **1:1 / square:** make the idea icon-like. Centered objects, circular type, a single punchline, and graphic framing work well; remove supporting copy that does not survive the crop.
- **Landscape:** stage a world around the product, create a before/after or left/right relationship, or use negative space for a headline. Avoid simply rotating a portrait layout.

### 5. Font system and multilingual control

Select a type family by role, not by novelty:

- **Condensed display sans:** urgency, flavor, sport, energy, and loud packaging.
- **Rounded or blobby display:** friendliness, youth, playful food/beverage, and soft products.
- **Editorial serif:** taste, coffee, premium food, culture, film, and literary mood.
- **Utility sans or mono:** specifications, benefits, dates, product facts, and engineered visual systems.
- **Script or expressive lettering:** warmth, invitation, craft, or a human voice; use sparingly so it remains special.

For multilingual posters, make each script feel native. Verify glyph coverage, line height, punctuation, numerals, diacritics, and whether the visual weight matches the Latin companion. A mixed-script lockup is a composition problem as much as a translation problem.

### Additional composition patterns from the new references

- **Type behind the hero:** giant words establish the field while the product interrupts them.
- **Ingredient architecture:** fruit, pasta, berries, ice, or cream become structural elements rather than props placed around a can.
- **System poster:** headline, annotations, badges, side labels, and bottom metadata share rules for alignment, spacing, and color.
- **Material window:** a tear, cut, or mask reveals the flavor or ingredient world inside the package.
- **Commercial surrealism:** keep the product label and silhouette accurate while changing the world around it.
